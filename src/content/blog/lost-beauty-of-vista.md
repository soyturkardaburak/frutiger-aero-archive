It has become easy to say something kind about Windows Vista, and the kindness can get syrupy. The system asked too often for permission. It was heavy. The gap between the Longhorn story and the box on the shelf was a real gap, not a myth invented by nostalgia. People who went back to XP were not philistines. They were using their computers.

The interface is still the most interesting thing Microsoft put on a default desktop in that decade. On 30 January 2007, if your graphics chip agreed, the window stopped being a painted frame and became a composited sheet. You could see the wallpaper in the title bar, softly. Segoe UI opened the letters up. Icons looked like they had been lit together. None of this required you to like the rest of the operating system. It did require you to admit that the chrome was trying to be a material, which XP’s Luna theme, for all its blue curves, never quite was.

Windows 7 is the version people defend without embarrassment, and it deserves that. It is also an edit of Vista’s idea. When I say the beauty of Vista got lost, I mean two losses. One is historical: the bad reputation covered the design so thoroughly that “Vista” became a punch line and the glass was credited to the sequel. The other is literal: Windows 8 retired the glass as the product’s face, and the material stopped being ordinary. Ordinary was the condition that made it invisible enough to miss later.

This site draws windows instead of capturing them. A screenshot of Vista is Microsoft’s picture, or the picture of whoever photographed their own screen, and it does not become mine because I have opinions about it. The opinion is enough to put here. The glass was a serious piece of design. The system around it was allowed to be flawed. Both sentences can sit on the same title bar.
