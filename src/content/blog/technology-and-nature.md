I can describe the picture without having the right to reprint it. There is a sky of an almost chemical blue. The grass is young. A building, if there is one, is white and curved and seems to have no loading dock. Water nearby is clear. A device is either in someone’s hand or so strongly implied that the landscape itself is the device. This arrangement was the pastoral of consumer electronics for a few years, and the University of Waterloo’s exhibit is right to call the mood a green future. It is a future in which the machine has been forgiven in advance.

The forgiveness is the part I keep turning over. A phone in a meadow does not tell you about mines, or about the electricity, or about the desk where the phone will actually live. It tells you that the company would like the object to feel as harmless as weather. Glossy interfaces did the same job indoors. A glass window frame is also a claim about cleanliness. Nature in the ad and glass on the desktop are two rooms in one argument: technology had become gentle.

It had not, not in any complete way. That does not make the pictures worthless, any more than a landscape painting is worthless because the county in it had politics. It does mean a revival can become cute if it forgets the sales pitch. I do not want Aero Atlas to be cute. I want it to be affectionate and specific. The affection is for the light, the particular green, the belief that a horizon was a reasonable background for a user interface. The specificity is that this was advertising and stock photography and operating-system art, produced by people with deadlines, not a folk style that grew out of the grass.

When I draw a pavilion for the gallery, the caption says it is a drawing. The real campaigns can stay in the magazines and in the archives that have the right to hold them. The theory of the future is what I can write down without borrowing the photograph. For a while, the theory was: the machine belongs outside, and the outside has been waiting for it, beautifully lit.
