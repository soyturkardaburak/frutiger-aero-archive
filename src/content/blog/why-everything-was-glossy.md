A highlight is a small lie that everyone agrees to. The light is not in the room. The button is not wet. The logo does not stand on a floor. And yet, for several years, a control without that highlight looked unfinished, as if the designer had left before the last pass.

Some of the reason is technical and not very romantic. Icons had grown up. A Vista-era symbol could be large, and a large symbol with a flat fill looks empty. A gradient and a specular spot give the extra pixels a job. Transparent images let a web page fake the same job without a compositor. Once the tools made the highlight easy — and they did, down to browser-based button makers — it became rude not to use it. Defaults spread faster than manifestos.

Some of the reason is tactile. Gloss reads as clean, new, and a little bit medical. A matte grey bezel reads as office equipment from the previous decade, which is what Luna and Aqua had already been trying to escape. Water, glass, and a wiped surface all say the object has not been used hard. That is a strange promise for a tool, and it is exactly the promise consumer technology wanted to make while it was still delighted to be in the house.

I do not think everyone was copying Windows. Aqua was earlier. Phone software was off imitating leather, which is a different material with a different moral. The shared habit was the light from the upper left and the belief that a computer should show its finish. When flat design arrived it did not ban beauty. It banned that particular proof. A sign did not need to look recently polished. The polishing, looking back, is what I miss, and also what I find slightly funny: so much effort, so that a button could appear to have been touched by a cloth.
