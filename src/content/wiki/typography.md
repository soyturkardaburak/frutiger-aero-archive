## The face in the nickname

Adrian Frutiger designed Frutiger for the wayfinding of Charles de Gaulle Airport. It was released in 1976. The letters are humanist: the strokes vary a little, the shapes open up, the counters are large enough to read while you walk. CARI’s definition of the aesthetic starts from that face, and from the observation that Windows Vista’s default interface type, Segoe UI, comes out of the same broad tradition. Segoe was designed under Steve Matteson and is documented in Microsoft’s own font list as the Vista-era interface family. It is not a copy of Frutiger, and this archive does not need it to be. The nickname is about a family resemblance and a shared mood: friendly, open, modern without being geometric and cold.

## The other faces in the room

Myriad, by Robert Slimbach and Carol Twombly for Adobe, was the humanist sans of a great deal of 1990s and 2000s technology branding. Calibri, by Lucas de Groot, became the default of Office 2007 and so the face of ordinary documents at the same moment Vista was teaching Segoe UI. On the web, designers could not assume any of those were installed. A careful stylesheet still ended in Tahoma, Verdana, Trebuchet MS, and Arial. Tahoma and Verdana are Matthew Carter’s screen faces. Trebuchet MS, by Vincent Connare, had been a web and Windows face since the 1990s and remained a favorite for headings that wanted to feel a little more drawn.

Aero Atlas sets its own pages in that practical stack: Segoe UI first, then Tahoma, Trebuchet MS, Verdana, and Arial. On a current Windows machine the first face is the one Vista would have used. On another system the fallbacks are the ones a 2008 stylesheet would have trusted. That is a small piece of historical manners.

## Type as part of the weather

The glossy aesthetic is usually discussed as a surface. The letters are half of the name for a reason. Humanist sans-serifs made the glass feel less like a machine room and more like a public sign. They are the typography of airports, of consumer electronics, and of interfaces that would like to be thought of as friendly. Geometric faces of the later flat period — and the very thin weights that came with high-density screens — tell a different story, closer to a poster than to a conversation. When the shine left, the letters changed temperature too.
