## Collecting a future that did not quite happen

The revival is not a return of Windows Aero as a default theme. It is a decision to look again at pictures that, for a few years, had seemed merely dated. Flat design had made them visible by replacing them. CARI had made them searchable by naming them in 2017. Through the early 2020s, image boards, short video, and personal archives started gathering the wallpapers, the stock skies, the glassy UI, and the green advertising as a single mood. The University of Waterloo Computer Museum put up an exhibit. Fan sites wrote timelines. A page like this one is part of that second life, and it should admit as much.

## What people are nostalgic for

Some of the longing is technical. Glass, blur, and a rich icon were signs that a computer had power to spare. Some of it is biographical: the style coincides with childhoods and teenage years spent on Vista-era PCs and early phones. Some of it is a judgment on what came after. Subscription software, flat brand systems, and interfaces that all share one sans-serif can make the older pictures look generous. They were also salesmanship. The meadow in the advertisement was not a promise the company knew how to keep. A good revival essay holds both thoughts. The journal on this site tries to.

## The duties of a late archive

A nostalgic collection can smuggle in three mistakes. It can pretend the name was used at the time. It can paste copyrighted screenshots and call the result research. It can invent a smoother history than the products deserved — Vista’s reputation included. Aero Atlas is built against those mistakes. The coinage is dated to 2017. Drawings are labeled as drawings. The disappointing chapters stay in the timeline.

The pleasure is allowed to stay too. A soap bubble on a blue field is allowed to be beautiful without being asked to represent a whole decade’s politics. The point of a shelf is that you can take one object down, look at it, and put it back beside the others.
