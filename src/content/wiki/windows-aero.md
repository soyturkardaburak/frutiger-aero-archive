## A theme with a compositor behind it

Windows Aero is the visual style Microsoft shipped with Windows Vista and kept, in a calmer form, for Windows 7. The part people remember is the frame: a translucent border, a highlight along the top edge, and the wallpaper faintly visible through the chrome. That effect was not a bitmap stretched behind the title text. The Desktop Window Manager composited the windows. Microsoft’s own overview of DWM describes a desktop whose off-screen surfaces can be transformed, blended, and shown with effects. Aero Glass is the famous one.

The drawing at the top of this article is an Aero Atlas study of that feeling. It is not a capture of the operating system. Real screenshots live in the interface archives linked from the resources page, where the copyright stays with the person who made the capture.

## What the word was asked to mean

Microsoft presented Aero as Authentic, Energetic, Reflective, and Open. The product guide, quoted in later writing on glass interfaces, talked about transparent frames, dynamic reflections, and smooth animation, and argued that the glass let you look at your work instead of at the window. Whether the frame really got out of the way is a fair argument. It certainly announced that the computer was new. A machine that could not run the compositor fell back to a plainer theme. The shine was, among other things, a hardware test you could see.

## What else Aero included

Glass frames were the poster. The theme also covered animation: windows that opened with a little motion, taskbar thumbnails of live windows, and, in Windows 7, Aero Peek, which faded other windows back so you could glance at the desktop, plus Shake and Snap. Flip 3D, the stacked window switcher in Vista, was the most literal “this desktop is a place” gesture. It was also easy to turn off and easy to forget. The durable idea was the material, not the carousel.

## Aero is not the whole aesthetic

Later writers borrowed Aero as the second half of “Frutiger Aero,” because the glass is the clearest single artifact of the wider glossy period. The borrowing can swallow too much. A rounded Web 2.0 badge is not Aero. A skeuomorphic phone calendar is not Aero. Aqua, on the Macintosh, is earlier and belongs to another company. Aero is the Windows theme. The climate around it — skies, grass, humanist type, reflective logos — is the larger subject of this encyclopedia. Keeping the theme’s proper name intact makes the later nickname easier to trust.
