## A reaction you can date

Flat design, in the mainstream, has a paper trail. Windows Phone 7 launched in Europe on 21 October 2010 and in the United States in November, with large type and tiles instead of reflective icons. Windows 8 brought that language to the PC on 26 October 2012 and stopped treating Aero Glass as the face of Windows. iOS 7 shipped on 18 September 2013 and removed the stitched and wooden textures from Apple’s phone. Google introduced Material Design in June 2014: still a system with light and shadow, now explained as paper and ink rather than as water and glass. The glossy climate did not die on one Tuesday. It lost its biggest stages across those four years.

## What flat was arguing

The practical argument was size. Phones, browsers, and high-density screens wanted symbols that survived being drawn at many scales and in one color. A gel orb is a small illustration. A flat glyph is closer to a character. The cultural argument was taste. Reflections had become the default way to say “modern,” which is how a default stops working. Skeuomorphic texture had the same problem. Once everyone knew what a notes application was, the paper was costume.

Flat design was never a single specification. Metro’s tiles, Apple’s thin type and bright planes, and Material’s stacked paper are different rooms. They share an unwillingness to look like Vista. That shared refusal is what turned the previous decade into an identifiable object. You can see a style clearly when the industry has agreed to stop.

## What it made possible, including this site

Without the flattening, Frutiger Aero would be a harder thing to name. A style that is still the water you swim in does not feel like an aesthetic; it feels like the present. The 2017 coinage arrives after the present has moved. Collectors in the 2020s are not asking Windows to bring the glass back as a default, at least not in the pages gathered here. They are asking to look at it on purpose. Flat design is part of that story because it closed the door.
