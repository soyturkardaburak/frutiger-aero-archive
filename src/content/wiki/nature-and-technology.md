## A picnic where the product should be

The most reliable picture of the period is not a dialog box. It is a horizon. Blue sky, a few soft clouds, grass that is greener than most lawns, water clear enough to see a fish, sometimes an aurora, sometimes a white building with no visible door. A phone, a laptop, or only the suggestion of one sits in that weather as if it grew there. CARI’s motif list — aurora, bokeh, macro grass, glass — reads like the keyword field of a stock library from the same years. The Waterloo exhibit describes the pairing of nature and futuristic architecture as a utopian green future. That phrase fits the ads better than it fits the supply chain behind them.

## What the weather was selling

High-definition capture and high-definition print gave advertisers more detail than they had in the late 1990s, and they spent it on texture: dew, skin, ripples, lens flare. The extra clarity was itself the message. A sharp blade of grass said the product was modern. The absence of offices, roads, and server rooms said the product was innocent. Eco-futurism, in this commercial form, did not have to explain energy use. It had to look like the explanation had already happened, somewhere just past the pavilion.

This is also why the images date so precisely even when the logo is cropped off. A muddy scan of a 1998 brochure does not look like this. A flat 2016 brand system, all solid color and simple geometry, does not either. The 2000s picture wants atmosphere and a product that does not disturb it.

## What this archive will and will not show

Aero Atlas does not reproduce campaign stills. Those photographs are someone’s copyright, and a fan encyclopedia does not become a museum by ignoring that. The drawings in the gallery are studies of the arrangement: sky, lawn, glass, a slogan written here rather than copied. When a Commons photograph is added, the licensing page will name the photographer. Until then, the horizon on this site is an illustration, and it is labeled as one.

The habit is still worth documenting, because it shaped what people thought “the future of technology” looked like. Not a command line. Not a grey box. A bright day, and a device polite enough to belong in it.
