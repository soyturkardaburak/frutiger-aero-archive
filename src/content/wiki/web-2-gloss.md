## A conference, an essay, and a look that tagged along

In October 2004 O’Reilly Media and MediaLive held a conference called Web 2.0. On 30 September 2005 Tim O’Reilly published the essay that tried to say what the phrase meant: the web as a platform, rich interfaces in the browser, users who contribute data, and software that gets better because people use it. You can read the whole piece and never find a specification for a gel button. The visual fashion arrived beside the argument and then, for a lot of readers, replaced it.

Personal sites and startup homepages of the next few years converged on a small kit. Corners grew round. Logos sat on a faint mirrored copy of themselves. Headings used a bright gradient. A “beta” ribbon implied that the site was alive and unfinished in a flattering way. Badges accumulated in the footer: valid markup, browsers, feeds. The feed icon, a white radio wave on an orange square, is one of the few marks from that kit that stayed useful after the gloss went away. This archive still publishes a real feed, for that reason.

## Why it looked the way it did

A 1024-pixel screen, newly common transparent PNGs, and a generation of button-making tools made the kit easy. Gloss read as “not the grey intranet.” It also read as friendly, which mattered for sites that were asking strangers to upload their lives. The same years gave Windows its glass and gave phone software its fake leather. On the web the material was usually cheaper: a gradient and a highlight, not a composited blur. Calling all of it Aero flattens that difference. Calling all of it Frutiger Aero is a later convenience. At the time it was just how a new service announced itself.

## What the museum sites are for

The Web Design Museum and the Wayback Machine are better witnesses than any recreation, including this one. A capture from 2007 shows the actual type sizes, the actual badge row, and the actual habit of centering a fixed-width page on a repeating sky. Aero Atlas borrows that skeleton on purpose — a column near 1100 pixels, panels with title bars, a glossy strip of links — and then fills it with writing that those sites did not yet have, because the style had not been given a retrospective name.

## The end of the costume

Flat design did not refute O’Reilly’s essay. It changed the costume. Once Windows 8 and iOS 7 had made reflections look dated, a startup homepage in gel buttons looked like a costume from a specific party. The ideas about platforms remained. The shine was what people meant when they said, a little unkindly, “very Web 2.0.”
